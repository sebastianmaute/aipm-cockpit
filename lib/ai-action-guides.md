# AI Action Guides

Built-in operating guides for the single-request AI actions (§701). Each `##` section becomes one built-in guide, sent only to the actions its `<!-- targets: ... -->` marker names. They describe method, not app behaviour: what each action may write is fixed by its own tool and preview, and nothing here can widen it. Users can switch any of them off, or reprioritise them, in Settings → Operating guides.

## Work breakdown method
<!-- targets: wbs -->

- Apply the 100% rule: the pieces of a split together cover all of the original task's work and nothing outside it. Do not add a piece for work that belongs to another task.
- Size work so it can be tracked: a task between about one day and two weeks of effort is easy to estimate and to report on. Split what is larger or vague; leave alone what is already small and clear.
- Break down by deliverable or outcome, not by person or department. Name each task as a verb plus the thing it produces ("Draft cutover runbook"), so finished means something checkable.
- Plan in rolling waves: detail the near-term work, keep work months away coarse. Do not split far-future tasks into steps nobody can foresee yet.
- Propose a missing task only when the plan clearly lacks work it needs (a test, a review, a handover, an approval) — not to restate work an existing task already covers.
- Add a finish-to-start link only for a hard dependency: the later task genuinely cannot start before the earlier one finishes. Do not link tasks merely because they are in sequence by habit.
- Estimate effort, not elapsed time, and include review and rework. When the information is thin, prefer no estimate over a guess.

## Resource allocation method
<!-- targets: alloc -->

- Plan to a sustainable load: allocate people to about 80–85% of their available time, so meetings, support and the unplanned have room. Never plan anyone above 100%.
- Respect availability first: absences, part-time patterns and existing commitments come before new work.
- Match work to skill and role, then balance load. A person who is the only one with a skill is a single point of failure; spread that work or flag it rather than piling it on them.
- Prefer continuity: keep a person on the work they already carry unless the load or a skill gap forces a change, since every hand-over costs time.
- Allocate scarce people to the critical path and the nearest deadlines first.
- When demand exceeds capacity, say so plainly rather than hiding it in over-allocation; the plan should show the gap.

## Steering committee reporting
<!-- targets: committeeReport -->

- Lead with the bottom line: overall status and the one or two things the committee must know, before any detail.
- State status against the plan, with evidence: what was due, what was delivered, what moved and why. Avoid adjectives without numbers.
- Separate decisions the committee must take from information it should note. For each decision, give the options, a recommendation and what happens if nothing is decided.
- Report risks and issues by impact on outcomes, with an owner and the next step — not as a list of everything open.
- Keep it short and honest: a red status raised early is worth more than a green one that turns red at the next meeting.

## Project setup method
<!-- targets: projectProposal -->

- Start from the outcome: a project proposal names what will be different when it is done, for whom, and how that will be checked.
- Organise the plan by phase or deliverable, with a milestone at each point where a decision or acceptance happens.
- Keep the first version lean: the work clearly needed to reach the first milestones, in reasonable detail, and later phases coarse.
- Make dates plausible: leave room for review, approval and handover, and do not compress every phase to its minimum.
- Name the obvious risks and the stakeholders who decide, so the project starts with them visible.

## RACI method
<!-- targets: raci -->

- Exactly one Accountable per item: the one person who answers for the outcome and signs it off. Never zero, never two.
- At least one Responsible per item: whoever does the work. The Accountable may also be Responsible on small items.
- Consult those whose input changes the result, before the work is done; inform those who need to know after. Keep both lists short — consulting everyone slows everything.
- Assign roles to people who hold the matching authority or skill; do not make someone Accountable for what they cannot decide.
- Watch the load: a person who is Accountable for most items is a bottleneck worth pointing out.

## Weekly digest narrative
<!-- targets: digest -->

- Write for a busy reader: what changed this week, what needs attention, and what comes next, in that order.
- Lead with the most significant change, positive or negative, and say why it matters.
- Use the figures given; do not invent numbers, dates or causes the data does not show.
- Keep a calm, factual tone. Name a problem plainly, without alarm and without softening it away.

## Insight recommendation method
<!-- targets: insightRecommend -->

- Recommend the smallest action that addresses the cause, not only the symptom.
- Prefer acting within the project's own levers (re-plan, reassign, re-scope, clarify) before escalating; escalate when the decision sits above the project or the impact crosses its tolerances.
- Make each recommendation concrete: who does what, by when, and how the effect will be visible.
- Say what the recommendation trades off — time, cost, scope or quality — so it can be judged.

## Duplicate detection method
<!-- targets: dedup -->

- Treat tasks as duplicates when they describe the same piece of work with the same outcome, not merely similar wording or the same topic.
- Different phases of one deliverable (draft, review, approve) are separate tasks, not duplicates.
- When merging, keep the version with the clearer name, the more complete description and the existing links and history.
- When in doubt, do not propose the merge: a missed duplicate costs a minute, a wrong merge loses work.
